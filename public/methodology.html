Where the figures come from

  • Every figure comes from the company's own filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), through SEC EDGAR's free XBRL data: the annual reports (10-K for US companies, 20-F and 40-F for foreign ones).
  • Each metric keeps one full-year value per fiscal year. When a later report restates a year, the latest filed value is used.
  • Companies tag the same item differently (revenue has several tags, for example), so alternative tags are tried in turn, year by year.
  • Per-share figures are adjusted for stock splits; a foreign company's figures stay in its reporting currency.
  • The SEC filing history comes from the company's filing list (8-K items, late-filing notices, amendments, SEC comment letters); insider trades from its Form 4 filings.
  • Results are stored and re-checked against SEC once a day; "Data as of" on each page says when.

Growth and trends

  • Growth per year (CAGR): the steady yearly growth that turns the first value into the latest one. It can't be worked out from a loss or a zero, so it then shows n/a with the reason.
  • Trend labels: "strong growth" at 10% a year or more, "growing" at 3% or more, "flat" between -3% and 3%, "declining" below -3%. "Volatile" is added when the figure fell by more than 2% in three or more years, and "had losses" when any year was negative.
  • Ratios over the whole period (margins, returns, debt against earnings) are total ÷ total across the years, so one odd year can't decide a test.

My portfolio and S&P 500 measures

Green and red use simple yardsticks: growth of 7% a year (5% for dividends and book value) or more is green, shrinking is red; margins and returns at 10-15% or more are green; for shares, buybacks are green and dilution of over 2% a year is red; for the Buffett and balance-sheet columns, three quarters or more met is green and under half is red; for the Piotroski F-score, 8 or 9 is green and 0-2 red, his own bands. They're a reading aid, not a verdict.

Choices we made

Where a source gives no exact number, or the filings can't give what it asks for, we had to choose. These are our choices, not the authors':

  • Graham's size test uses $750 million of revenue (his $100 million from 1972, adjusted for inflation), and his 20-year dividend record becomes the 10 years available here.
  • Buffett's tests are common numerical readings of his letters (15% returns, 7% earnings growth, debt within 4 years of earnings, and so on); Buffett himself rarely gives hard numbers.
  • The value estimate discounts free cash flow per share at 10%, with growth capped at 12% for 10 years and 3% after; you can change all three on the Graham & Buffett tab.
  • Owner earnings are taken as free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending), the usual reading of Buffett's 1986 definition; their yield is the latest year's per share ÷ the price. EV / free cash flow uses the price × shares outstanding, plus total debt, minus cash, over the latest free cash flow, on the Compare page, and isn't shown for banks.
  • Lynch's growth is the last 5 years' EPS growth, not expected growth; his price tests don't apply above 25% a year, and growth plus yield over P/E passes at 1.5.
  • "Rising steadily" means EPS, or retained earnings, rose in at least 7 of every 10 years.
  • R&D counts as a modest burden under 30% of gross profit.
  • The colour yardsticks on My portfolio and the S&P 500 picks are ours.
